A digital planner is just a PDF you write on in an app like GoodNotes,
Notability, or Xodo — but a *good* one is engineered so you never lose your
place. Here's what separates a planner you'll actually use from a pretty file
you'll abandon in week two.

## 1. Real hyperlinking

This is the single biggest quality difference. In a well-built planner, tapping
a date jumps you to that day, and a "home" button is always one tap away.
Without hyperlinks you're scrolling through hundreds of pages, and that
friction is exactly why people quit. Before you commit to any planner, check
that the monthly, weekly, and daily pages are actually linked together.

## 2. Layouts that match how you study

- **Weekly spreads** for seeing the whole week of classes and deadlines.
- **Daily pages** for time-blocking study sessions.
- **Subject/assignment trackers** so grades and due dates aren't scattered.

You don't need 40 layout variations — you need the two or three you'll open
every day.

## 3. App compatibility

Any real digital planner is a PDF, so it works in GoodNotes, Notability, Xodo,
and most PDF annotation apps on iPad, Android tablets, or Windows with a
stylus. If a "planner" only works in one proprietary app, that's a red flag.

## 4. Undated (or re-dated) pages

Undated planners let you start any time of year and skip weeks without wasting
pages — much better value than a dated planner that's useless by March.

## Paper vs digital, briefly

If you're deciding between formats, we compared them here:
[Notion vs paper planners for students](/articles/notion-vs-paper-planner-students/).
A digital planner sits in the middle — the tactile feel of writing by hand,
with the searchability and reusability of a file.
